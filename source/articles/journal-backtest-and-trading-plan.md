# The trading journal, backtesting and the trading plan

**Covers:** trading journal, backtesting, win rate, trading plan, entry rule, exit rule, trading strategy

## Turning trading into something you can improve {#overview}
Try to remember your last ten trades without looking anything up. Most people recall the good ones as skill and the bad ones as bad luck, because memory edits the story. Written records and written rules replace that memory with evidence, and evidence tells you what to keep and what to drop. Let's go through the tools that do this.

## Trading journal {#trading-journal}
A trading journal is a log you write for every trade so that you can review it later. A good entry holds the stock, the time, the entry and exit prices, the size, why you took the trade, what you felt, and whether you followed your plan. A screenshot of the chart helps.

## Win rate {#win-rate}
Win rate is the share of your trades that ended as winners. If 45 of 100 trades won, it is 45%. On its own it tells you very little. A trader who wins 70% of the time can still lose money if the losers are much bigger than the winners, so read the win rate together with the average win and the average loss.

## Backtesting {#backtesting}
Backtesting means applying a set of trading rules to past prices to see how they would have done, before any real money is at stake. It is quick and carries no risk. It also has limits. The past does not repeat on command, and you can adjust the rules until they fit old data perfectly and then watch them fail on new data. Backtests also look better than real trading, because real trading has fees and emotions.

## Trading plan {#trading-plan}
A trading plan is a document in which you fix the rules in advance: which stocks, which entries and exits, how much risk, and how you will review the results. You write it while calm, and you follow it while under pressure.

## Trading strategy {#trading-strategy}
A trading strategy is the main idea behind how you look for opportunities, for example buying breakouts, or buying dips back to a moving average. It says what kind of situation you hunt for. The plan is wider: it contains the strategy plus the risk limits, the schedule and the review rules.

## Entry rule {#entry-rule}
An entry rule is a specific, written condition that must be true before you open a trade. For example: "the stock is above its 50-day average, and the price closes above yesterday's high."

## Exit rule {#exit-rule}
An exit rule is a specific, written condition that tells you when to close a trade, whether it is winning or losing. For example: "sell at 3 times my risk, or if the price closes below the entry candle's low."

## A worked example {#example}
A trader writes a plan with one strategy: buy pullbacks in an uptrend.

- **Entry rule:** the stock is above its 50-day average, it has pulled back to the 20-day average, and a bullish candle closes above the previous candle's high.
- **Exit rules:** the stop sits below the pullback low, the target is twice the risk, and the trade ends by the close of the day.
- **Risk:** 1% of equity per trade, and a stop for the day after 2% is lost.

She backtests 100 setups from last year by hand on charts. The result is 45 winners and 55 losers, so the win rate is 45 / 100 = 45%. Each winner earned 2 units and each loser lost 1 unit. The total is 45 × 2 − 55 × 1 = 90 − 55 = 35 units, an average of 0.35 units per trade. That is positive. If one unit is 100 dollars, it is 35 dollars per trade before costs. With costs of 5 dollars a trade, it is 30 dollars, still positive.

Now compare a trader with a 70% win rate and a poor ratio: winners earn 1 unit and losers lose 3 units. Over 100 trades the total is 70 × 1 − 30 × 3 = 70 − 90 = −20 units. A high win rate, and a losing system.

Back to our trader. She trades the plan with a small size and keeps a journal. After 50 live trades, her review shows:

| Question | Answer from the journal |
|---|---|
| Win rate | 40% |
| Average result | +0.1 units |
| Trades that broke the plan | 12 of 50 |
| Result of trades that broke the plan | −9 units |
| Result of trades that followed it | +14 units |

The journal shows what the win rate hides. The 12 trades outside the plan lost 9 units, and the 38 trades inside it earned 14. The net is 14 − 9 = 5 units over 50 trades, which is the 0.1 average. Repairing her discipline is worth more than changing the strategy.

A plan is never finished in one sitting. Review it every month, change one thing at a time, and write down why you changed it.

## A common mistake {#mistake}
A common slip is to backtest until the rules fit the past perfectly, and then to trust the result. That is fitting noise. Test on one period, then check on a different period you have not looked at. Another slip is a journal that records results but never the reasons. You cannot learn much from it afterwards.

## Key points {#key-points}
- A journal records the setup, the reasoning, the result and the feelings for each trade.
- Win rate means little without the size of wins and losses.
- Backtesting tests rules on the past, and it flatters them.
- A plan holds the strategy, the entry and exit rules and the risk limits, and you follow it as written.
