# The trading journal, backtesting and the trading plan

**Covers:** trading journal, backtesting, win rate, trading plan, entry rule, exit rule, trading strategy

## Turning trading into something you can improve {#overview}
Without records, trading is a series of memories that the mind edits. A good day is remembered as skill, and a bad day as bad luck. Records and written rules replace the memory with evidence, and the evidence shows what to keep and what to drop.

## Trading journal {#trading-journal}
A trading journal is a written log in which a trader records each trade's setup, reasoning, result and lessons, to review later. A good entry includes the stock, the time, the entry and exit prices, the size, the reason for the trade, what you felt, and whether you followed the plan. Screenshots of the chart help.

## Win rate {#win-rate}
Win rate is the percentage of a trader's trades that ended as winners, out of all trades taken. Alone, it says little: a trader who wins 70% of the time but loses much more on the losers can still lose money. It must be read together with the size of the average win and the average loss.

## Backtesting {#backtesting}
Backtesting means testing a set of trading rules against past price data to see how it would have performed before ever risking real money. It is quick and free of risk, but has limits: the past is not the future, and rules can be tuned until they fit old data perfectly and then fail on new data. Backtesting results look better than real results, because real trading has fees and emotions.

## Trading plan {#trading-plan}
A trading plan is the document in which a trader fixes the rules in advance: which stocks, which entries and exits, what size of risk, and how results are reviewed. It is written when calm, and followed when under pressure.

## Trading strategy {#trading-strategy}
A trading strategy is the core idea of how you look for opportunities, for example buying breakouts or buying dips back to a moving average. It describes what kind of situation you look for. The plan is wider: it contains the strategy plus risk limits, schedule and review rules.

## Entry rule {#entry-rule}
An entry rule is a specific, written condition in the plan that must be true before opening a trade. For example: "the stock is above its 50-day average, and the price closes above yesterday's high."

## Exit rule {#exit-rule}
An exit rule is a specific, written condition that decides when to close a trade, whether winning or losing. For example: "sell at 3 times my risk, or if the price closes below the entry candle's low."

## A worked example {#example}
A trader writes a plan with one strategy: buy pullbacks in an uptrend.

- **Entry rule:** the stock is above its 50-day average; it has pulled back to the 20-day average; a bullish candle closes above the previous candle's high.
- **Exit rules:** the stop is just below the pullback low; the target is twice the risk; exit by the close of the day.
- **Risk:** 1% of equity per trade; stop after 2% lost in a day.

She backtests 100 setups from last year, by hand on charts. Results: 45 winners, 55 losers. Win rate = 45 / 100 = 45%. Each winner earned 2 units, each loser lost 1 unit. Total = 45 × 2 − 55 × 1 = 90 − 55 = 35 units. The average per trade is 0.35 units, positive. If one unit is 100 dollars, that is 35 dollars per trade before costs. With costs of 5 dollars a trade, it is 30 dollars, still positive.

Compare a trader with a 70% win rate, but a bad ratio: winners earn 1 unit, losers lose 3 units. Total for 100 trades = 70 × 1 − 30 × 3 = 70 − 90 = −20 units. A high win rate, a losing system.

Then she trades the plan with a small size and keeps a journal. After 50 live trades, a review shows:

| Question | Answer from the journal |
|---|---|
| Win rate | 40% |
| Average result | +0.1 units |
| Trades that broke the plan | 12 of 50 |
| Result of trades that broke the plan | −9 units |
| Result of trades that followed it | +14 units |

The journal reveals something the win rate hides: the 12 trades outside the plan lost 9 units, while the 38 within the plan earned 14. Fixing discipline is worth more than fixing the strategy.

A plan is not finished once. Review it every month, change only one thing at a time, and write down why.

## A common mistake {#mistake}
Backtesting until the rules fit the past perfectly, and trusting the result. That is fitting noise. Test on one period, then check on another that you have not looked at. Another mistake is writing a journal that only records results and never the reasons, which makes review useless.

## Key points {#key-points}
- A journal records setup, reasoning, result and feelings for each trade.
- Win rate means little without the size of wins and losses.
- Backtesting tests rules on the past, and flatters them.
- A plan holds the strategy, entry and exit rules, and risk limits, and is followed as written.
