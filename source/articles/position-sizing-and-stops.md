# Position sizing, stop-loss and risk-reward

**Covers:** position size, account equity, risk per trade, stop-loss, take-profit, risk-reward ratio

## Deciding how much, before deciding what {#overview}
When you start, most of your attention goes to choosing what to buy. Experienced traders spend more of it on how much to buy and where to get out. Those two choices decide whether a bad streak is survivable. A good idea with a bad size can end an account, while a mediocre idea with a good size can last for years. Let's build the idea one number at a time.

## Account equity {#account-equity}
Account equity is what your trading account is worth right now: the cash plus the market value of your open positions, minus anything you borrowed. It changes after every trade, and every rule below takes it as the starting point.

## Risk per trade {#risk-per-trade}
Risk per trade is the dollar amount you accept losing if one trade goes wrong. It is usually set as a small percentage of account equity, and many traders choose 1% or less. The purpose is to let you survive a long losing streak.

## Stop-loss {#stop-loss}
A stop-loss is an order you place in advance that sells the stock automatically if it falls to a price you chose. It caps the loss on that trade. The point is to decide the exit before the trade, while you are calm, and not during it, when fear does the talking.

## Take-profit {#take-profit}
A take-profit is the same idea on the other side. It is an order that sells automatically when the stock rises to a chosen price, which locks in a planned gain. It removes the temptation to hold on for more.

## Position size {#position-size}
Position size is the number of shares, or the dollar amount, that you buy in one trade, chosen to match the risk you accept. It follows from the other numbers:

position size = risk per trade / (entry price − stop price)

## Risk-reward ratio {#risk-reward-ratio}
The risk-reward ratio compares what you stand to lose with what you stand to gain if the trade works. Risking 1 dollar to make 3 is a ratio of 1 to 3. The larger the gain compared with the loss, the less often you need to be right.

## A worked example {#example}
Your account equity is 20,000 dollars, and your risk per trade is 1%. Let's work through it.

1. Risk per trade = 20,000 × 0.01 = 200 dollars.
2. The plan is to buy at 50.00, with a stop-loss at 49.00 and a take-profit at 53.00.
3. Risk per share = 50.00 − 49.00 = 1.00.
4. Position size = 200 / 1.00 = 200 shares.
5. The position costs 200 × 50 = 10,000 dollars, half of the account, but the risk is only 200 dollars.
6. Reward per share = 53.00 − 50.00 = 3.00, so the reward is 600 dollars.
7. The risk-reward ratio is 1 to 3: risking 200 to make 600.

If the stop is hit, you lose 200, which is 1% of the account. If the target is hit, you gain 600, which is 3%.

How often must you be right to break even? With a ratio of 1 to 3, the break-even rate is 1 / (1 + 3) = 25%. Out of 100 trades, winning 25 earns 25 × 600 = 15,000, and losing 75 costs 75 × 200 = 15,000. The net is zero. A trader who wins 40 of 100 earns 40 × 600 − 60 × 200 = 24,000 − 12,000 = 12,000 dollars before costs.

Compare a ratio of 1 to 1. The break-even rate is 50%, so you must be right half the time to stay level, before any fees.

| Ratio | Break-even share of winning trades |
|---|---|
| 1 to 1 | 50% |
| 1 to 2 | 33% |
| 1 to 3 | 25% |

Now see what the risk percentage does. A string of 10 losses in a row, at 1% each, takes the account from 20,000 down to about 20,000 × 0.99^10 = 18,088, a loss of about 9.6%. That is painful and recoverable. If the risk were 10% per trade, the same streak would leave 20,000 × 0.9^10 = 6,974, a loss of 65%, and the account would have to gain about 187% to recover.

Do not move the stop further away once the trade is on. That is how a plan to lose 200 dollars turns into a loss of 600.

## A common mistake {#mistake}
A common slip is to choose the number of shares first ("I'll buy 500") and the stop second, so that the risk is whatever it turns out to be. Work in the other order: risk first, stop second, size last. Another slip is a stop that is too tight, inside the normal wiggle of the stock, so it triggers on every small move.

## Key points {#key-points}
- Set the risk per trade as a small percentage of account equity.
- Position size = risk per trade divided by the distance from entry to stop.
- A stop-loss caps the loss, a take-profit locks the gain, and the ratio compares the two.
- A better risk-reward ratio lowers the share of winning trades you need.
