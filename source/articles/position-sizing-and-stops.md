# Position sizing, stop-loss and risk-reward

**Covers:** position size, account equity, risk per trade, stop-loss, take-profit, risk-reward ratio

## Deciding how much, before deciding what {#overview}
Beginners spend their time choosing what to buy. Professionals spend it on how much to buy and where to get out. These choices decide whether a bad streak is survivable. A good idea with a bad size can end an account. A mediocre idea with a good size can survive for years.

## Account equity {#account-equity}
Account equity is the total current value of everything in your trading account: the cash plus the market value of the open positions, minus anything borrowed. It changes after every trade, and every rule below uses it as the base.

## Risk per trade {#risk-per-trade}
Risk per trade is the dollar amount you are willing to lose on a single trade if it goes wrong. It is usually set as a small percentage of account equity. Many traders choose 1% or less. The purpose is to survive a long losing streak.

## Stop-loss {#stop-loss}
A stop-loss is a preset order that automatically sells a stock when it falls to a chosen price, capping how much you can lose on that trade. The exit is decided before the trade, when your mind is calm, and not during it, when fear speaks.

## Take-profit {#take-profit}
A take-profit is a preset order that automatically sells a stock when it rises to a chosen price, locking in a planned gain. It removes the temptation to hold on for more.

## Position size {#position-size}
Position size is the number of shares, or the dollar amount, you buy in a single trade, chosen to match the risk you are willing to take. It follows from the other numbers:

position size = risk per trade / (entry price − stop price)

## Risk-reward ratio {#risk-reward-ratio}
The risk-reward ratio compares how much you stand to lose with how much you stand to gain if the trade works. Risking 1 dollar to make 3 is a ratio of 1 to 3. The wider the gain compared with the loss, the less often you need to be right.

## A worked example {#example}
Account equity: 20,000 dollars. Risk per trade: 1%.

1. Risk per trade = 20,000 × 0.01 = 200 dollars.
2. The plan: buy at 50.00, stop-loss at 49.00, take-profit at 53.00.
3. Risk per share = 50.00 − 49.00 = 1.00.
4. Position size = 200 / 1.00 = 200 shares.
5. The position costs 200 × 50 = 10,000 dollars, half of the account, but the risk is only 200 dollars.
6. Reward per share = 53.00 − 50.00 = 3.00, so the reward is 600 dollars.
7. The risk-reward ratio is 1 to 3: risking 200 to make 600.

If the stop is hit, the loss is 200 (1% of the account). If the target is hit, the gain is 600 (3%).

How often must you be right to break even? With a 1 to 3 ratio, the break-even rate is 1 / (1 + 3) = 25%. Out of 100 trades, winning 25 earns 25 × 600 = 15,000, and losing 75 costs 75 × 200 = 15,000. Net: zero. A trader who wins 40 trades in 100 would earn 40 × 600 − 60 × 200 = 24,000 − 12,000 = 12,000 dollars before costs.

Compare a 1 to 1 ratio: the break-even rate is 50%. The trader needs to be right half the time just to stay even, before any fees.

| Ratio | Break-even share of winning trades |
|---|---|
| 1 to 1 | 50% |
| 1 to 2 | 33% |
| 1 to 3 | 25% |

A string of 10 losses in a row, at 1% each, takes the account from 20,000 down to about 20,000 × 0.99^10 = 18,088, a loss of about 9.6%. It is painful and recoverable. If risk were 10% per trade, the same streak would leave 20,000 × 0.9^10 = 6,974, a loss of 65%, and the account would need to gain about 187% to recover.

Do not move the stop further away once the trade is on. That is how a 200 dollar plan becomes a 600 dollar loss.

## A common mistake {#mistake}
Choosing the number of shares first ("I'll buy 500") and the stop second, so that the risk is whatever it turns out to be. Do it the other way round. Another mistake is placing the stop too tight, inside the normal noise of the stock, so that it triggers on every wiggle.

## Key points {#key-points}
- Set the risk per trade as a small percentage of account equity.
- Position size = risk per trade divided by the distance from entry to stop.
- A stop-loss caps the loss, a take-profit locks the gain, and the ratio compares them.
- A better risk-reward ratio lowers the share of winning trades you need.
