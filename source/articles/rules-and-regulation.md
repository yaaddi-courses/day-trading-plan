# Who makes the rules: FINRA, the SEC and the day-trading margin rule

**Covers:** FINRA, SEC, pattern day trader, intraday margin requirement

## Markets have referees {#overview}
You may wonder who stops a broker from treating customers unfairly, or who decides how much you are allowed to borrow. The answer is regulation. Rules decide who may offer brokerage services, how orders must be handled, how much borrowing is allowed and what must be disclosed. You do not need to know every rule. You do need to know the ones that decide what your account can do, because breaking them can freeze it.

## SEC {#sec}
The SEC, the Securities and Exchange Commission, is the US government agency that oversees the stock markets and protects investors. It enforces the law against fraud and market manipulation, requires public companies to disclose their finances, and supervises the exchanges and the brokerage industry. As a federal agency, it can investigate and bring cases.

## FINRA {#finra}
FINRA, the Financial Industry Regulatory Authority, writes and enforces the rulebook that brokers must follow. It is a self-regulatory body, and the SEC oversees it. Its rules cover how brokers deal with customers, how much customers may borrow, and how accounts are handled. When your broker says "because of a regulation", the rule often comes from FINRA.

## Pattern day trader {#pattern-day-trader}
For many years, "pattern day trader" was the label for someone in a margin account who made four or more day trades within five business days. The label came with a requirement: the account had to hold at least 25,000 dollars in equity, and an account below that was restricted from further day trading. That was a FINRA rule from the past, and 2026 brought a change, which we look at next.

## Intraday margin requirement {#intraday-margin-requirement}
The intraday margin requirement is the 2026 FINRA rule that replaced the old 25,000 dollar minimum. In place of a fixed number, brokers must hold equity in proportion to how much a trader is actually exposed during the day. The more risk a trader takes intraday, the more equity the account must hold. FINRA and each broker set the details, such as exact percentages and start dates, so check the current rules directly.

## A worked example {#example}
Let's start with the old rule and a small margin account of 8,000 dollars.

1. In one week the trader makes five day trades in five business days. That is at least four, so the account is flagged as a pattern day trader.
2. The account holds 8,000, below the required 25,000. The broker restricts it, so new day trades are blocked until the equity is brought up or the restriction period ends.
3. The gap is 25,000 − 8,000 = 17,000 dollars. For a small trader, this was a hard barrier.

Beginners tried to get around it with a cash account, which has no limit on the number of trades but is held back by the settlement rule on reusing funds. Others kept the count at three day trades in five days.

Under the newer approach, the barrier is no longer one fixed sum. The requirement depends on exposure, so a trader who takes small positions and closes them quickly needs less equity than one who takes large positions. With 8,000 dollars, day trading in a margin account may become possible, with limits tied to size. We do not show exact numbers here, because they depend on the current rules and on the broker's own settings, which may be stricter.

| | Old pattern day trader rule | Intraday margin requirement |
|---|---|---|
| Based on | Count of day trades | Risk taken intraday |
| Fixed minimum | 25,000 dollars | None fixed |
| Set by | FINRA | FINRA, plus broker rules |

Whatever the rule says, three habits help. Read your broker's day-trading page. Know your exposure before you place an order. And keep a margin of safety, so that one loss does not push the account below its requirement.

## A common mistake {#mistake}
A common slip is to rely on an article, a video or a course that still describes the old 25,000 dollar rule. Rules change, and your broker's current page decides what applies to your account. Another slip is to think the SEC and FINRA are one body. The SEC is the government agency, and FINRA is the industry's self-regulatory organization that works under it.

## Key points {#key-points}
- The SEC is the federal regulator. FINRA writes and enforces rules for brokers.
- The pattern day trader label meant four or more day trades in five days, with a 25,000 dollar minimum.
- In 2026, an intraday margin requirement tied to actual exposure replaced that minimum.
- Check your broker's current rules before relying on any number.
