# Who makes the rules: FINRA, the SEC and the day-trading margin rule

**Covers:** FINRA, SEC, pattern day trader, intraday margin requirement

## Markets have referees {#overview}
Trading is a regulated activity. Rules decide who may offer brokerage services, how orders must be handled, how much borrowing is allowed and what must be disclosed. A trader does not need to know every rule, but must know the ones that decide what an account can do, since breaking them can freeze an account.

## SEC {#sec}
The SEC, the Securities and Exchange Commission, is the US government agency that regulates the stock markets and protects investors. It enforces the law against fraud and market manipulation, requires public companies to disclose their finances, and oversees the exchanges and the broker industry. It is a federal agency with the power to investigate and to bring cases.

## FINRA {#finra}
FINRA, the Financial Industry Regulatory Authority, is the organization that writes and enforces the rulebook that brokers must follow. It is a self-regulatory body, overseen by the SEC. Its rules cover how brokers deal with customers, how much customers may borrow, and how accounts are handled. When your broker says "because of a regulation", the rule often comes from FINRA.

## Pattern day trader {#pattern-day-trader}
Pattern day trader was, for many years, the label for a trader in a margin account who made four or more day trades within five business days. That label brought a requirement: the account had to hold at least 25,000 dollars in equity, and an account below that was restricted from further day trading. It was a historical FINRA rule, and 2026 brought a change, described next.

## Intraday margin requirement {#intraday-margin-requirement}
The intraday margin requirement is the 2026 FINRA rule that replaced the old 25,000 dollar minimum. Instead of a fixed number, brokers must hold equity in proportion to how much a trader is actually exposed during the day. The more risk a trader takes intraday, the more equity the account must hold. Details, such as exact percentages and start dates, are set by FINRA and by each broker, so the current rules should be checked directly.

## A worked example {#example}
Under the old rule, take a small account with 8,000 dollars in a margin account.

1. In one week, the trader makes five day trades, in five business days. That is at least four, so the account is flagged as a pattern day trader.
2. The account holds 8,000, below the required 25,000. The broker restricts it, so new day trades are blocked until the equity is brought up or the restriction period ends.
3. The gap is 25,000 − 8,000 = 17,000 dollars. For a small trader, this was a hard barrier.

Beginners tried to get around it with a cash account (no limit on number of trades, but the settlement rule limits reuse of funds), or by keeping the count at three day trades in five days.

Under the newer approach, the barrier is no longer one fixed sum. The requirement depends on exposure. A trader who takes small positions and closes them quickly would need less equity than one taking large positions. For a trader with 8,000 dollars, this may make day trading in a margin account possible, but with limits tied to size. The exact numbers are not shown here, because they depend on current rules and on the broker's own, possibly stricter, settings.

| | Old pattern day trader rule | Intraday margin requirement |
|---|---|---|
| Based on | Count of day trades | Risk taken intraday |
| Fixed minimum | 25,000 dollars | None fixed |
| Set by | FINRA | FINRA, plus broker rules |

Whatever the rule, three habits help. Read your broker's day-trading page. Know your exposure before you place an order. And keep a margin of safety, so that one loss does not push the account below its requirement.

## A common mistake {#mistake}
Relying on an article, a video or a course that still describes the old 25,000 dollar rule. Rules change, and the broker's current page decides what applies to your account. Another mistake is thinking that the SEC and FINRA are the same body. The SEC is the government agency, and FINRA is the industry's self-regulatory organization under it.

## Key points {#key-points}
- The SEC is the federal regulator. FINRA writes and enforces rules for brokers.
- The pattern day trader label meant four or more day trades in five days, with a 25,000 dollar minimum.
- In 2026, an intraday margin requirement tied to actual exposure replaced that minimum.
- Check the broker's current rules before relying on any number.
