# Brokers, account types, buying power and leverage

**Covers:** broker, cash account, margin account, buying power, leverage

## You cannot trade on an exchange directly {#overview}
Individuals do not send orders straight to a stock exchange. They go through a licensed firm. The type of account that you open with that firm decides whether you may borrow, and borrowing is the single feature that most increases both the profits and the dangers of day trading.

## Broker {#broker}
A broker is a licensed firm or platform, often an app, that you place stock orders through. It holds your money and your shares, sends your orders to the market, and reports what happened. Brokers are regulated, and in the United States your account is usually covered by investor protection against the broker's failure, though not against your own trading losses.

## Cash account {#cash-account}
A cash account lets you trade only with money you have actually deposited. There is no borrowing. If you have 5,000 dollars, you can buy up to 5,000 dollars of stock. Cash accounts also have a settlement rule: after you sell, the money is not available to trade again immediately, which limits how often you can reuse it.

## Margin account {#margin-account}
A margin account lets you borrow money from your broker to buy more stock than your own cash could afford. The shares you hold serve as security for the loan, and the broker charges interest on what you borrow. Margin accounts, the plural, also allow short selling, since selling borrowed shares needs the same arrangement.

## Buying power {#buying-power}
Buying power is the total dollar amount of stock you are allowed to purchase right now, including any money your broker lets you borrow. In a cash account, it equals your available cash. In a margin account, it can be a multiple of your cash. It is a limit set by the broker, not a recommendation.

## Leverage {#leverage}
Leverage is using borrowed money to control a bigger position than your own cash could buy. It multiplies both gains and losses by the same factor. With 2 to 1 leverage, a 1% move in the stock changes your equity by 2%.

## A worked example {#example}
You have 5,000 dollars. A stock trades at 50 dollars.

**Cash account.** Buying power is 5,000. You buy 100 shares.

**Margin account at 2 to 1.** Buying power is 10,000. You buy 200 shares, paying 5,000 of your own and borrowing 5,000.

The stock rises 4%, to 52. Compare:

| | Cash (100 shares) | Margin (200 shares) |
|---|---|---|
| Value change | 100 × 2 = +200 | 200 × 2 = +400 |
| Return on your 5,000 | +4% | +8% |

Now the stock falls 4%, to 48:

| | Cash | Margin |
|---|---|---|
| Value change | −200 | −400 |
| Return on your 5,000 | −4% | −8% |

The same move gives twice the profit and twice the loss. And margin has an extra cost: the interest on the borrowed 5,000. At an annual rate of, say, 10%, that is about 500 dollars per year, or about 2 dollars per day. For a day trade closed the same day, interest is often small or zero, but for positions held overnight, it adds up.

A larger fall shows the danger. If the stock fell 50%, the cash account loses 2,500 (half of its money). The margin account loses 200 × 25 = 5,000, which is all of your own money. You would owe the broker its loan back from your remaining cash, and the broker would long before then demand that you add funds or sell the position.

Leverage is a tool, not a goal. Many beginners start with a cash account, to learn without the multiplier. A margin account is a choice to be made with the rules and the numbers in front of you.

## A common mistake {#mistake}
Treating buying power as the amount one should trade. Buying power is the broker's upper limit, and using all of it leaves no room for a normal loss. Another mistake is not reading the margin agreement, which sets the interest rate and the conditions under which the broker can sell your shares.

## Key points {#key-points}
- A broker is the licensed firm that places your orders and holds your assets.
- A cash account trades only your own money. A margin account lets you borrow.
- Buying power is the broker's limit on what you may buy.
- Leverage multiplies gains and losses equally, and borrowing costs interest.
