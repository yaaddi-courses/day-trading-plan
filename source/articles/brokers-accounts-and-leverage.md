# Brokers, account types, buying power and leverage

**Covers:** broker, cash account, margin account, buying power, leverage

## How you reach the market {#overview}
You might picture buying a share as sending an order straight to the stock exchange. Individuals cannot do that. Your order goes through a licensed firm, and the kind of account you open with it decides one important thing: whether you may borrow. Borrowing is the feature that raises both the profits and the dangers of day trading the most, so let's go through it slowly.

## Broker {#broker}
A broker is the licensed firm, often an app, that places your orders for you. It keeps your money and your shares, sends each order to the market, and reports back what happened. Brokers are regulated. In the United States, an account is usually protected if the broker itself fails, though that protection does not cover losses from your own trades.

## Cash account {#cash-account}
In a cash account you trade only with money you have deposited. With 5,000 dollars you can buy up to 5,000 dollars of stock, and nothing more. There is also a settlement rule: after you sell, the money takes a little time to become available again, so you cannot reuse it instantly.

## Margin account {#margin-account}
A margin account lets you borrow from the broker so that you can buy more than your own cash would allow. The shares you hold act as security for the loan, and the broker charges interest on what you borrow. The same arrangement is needed for short selling, where you sell shares you have borrowed, so margin accounts permit that too.

## Buying power {#buying-power}
Buying power is the total amount of stock the broker will let you buy at this moment, borrowed money included. In a cash account it equals your available cash. In a margin account it can be a multiple of your cash. Notice that it is a limit set by the broker. It is not a suggestion about how much you should trade.

## Leverage {#leverage}
Leverage means using borrowed money to control a larger position than your own cash could buy. It multiplies gains and losses by the same factor. At 2 to 1, a 1% move in the stock changes your equity by 2%.

## A worked example {#example}
You have 5,000 dollars and a stock costs 50 dollars.

In a **cash account**, your buying power is 5,000, so you buy 100 shares.

In a **margin account at 2 to 1**, your buying power is 10,000, so you buy 200 shares. You pay 5,000 from your own cash and borrow the other 5,000.

Say the stock rises 4%, from 50 to 52. Each share gains 2 dollars.

| | Cash (100 shares) | Margin (200 shares) |
|---|---|---|
| Value change | 100 × 2 = +200 | 200 × 2 = +400 |
| Return on your 5,000 | +4% | +8% |

Now say it falls 4%, from 50 to 48. Each share loses 2 dollars.

| | Cash | Margin |
|---|---|---|
| Value change | −200 | −400 |
| Return on your 5,000 | −4% | −8% |

The same move gave you twice the profit and twice the loss. Margin also has a separate cost, the interest on the 5,000 you borrowed. At an annual rate of 10%, that is about 500 dollars a year, or about 1.40 dollars a day. For a trade closed the same day, interest is often small or nothing, but on positions held overnight it accumulates.

A bigger fall shows the real danger. If the stock dropped 50%, to 25, the cash account would lose 100 × 25 = 2,500, half of its money. The margin account would lose 200 × 25 = 5,000, which is all of your own money. In practice the broker would act long before that, by demanding more funds or selling the position for you.

Leverage is a tool, and the choice to use it should be made with the rules and the numbers in front of you. Many beginners start with a cash account so that they can learn without the multiplier.

## A common mistake {#mistake}
A common slip is to treat buying power as the amount you ought to trade. It is the broker's upper limit, and using all of it leaves no room for an ordinary losing trade. Another slip is skipping the margin agreement. It states the interest rate and the conditions under which the broker may sell your shares.

## Key points {#key-points}
- A broker is the licensed firm that places your orders and holds your assets.
- A cash account uses only your own money. A margin account lets you borrow.
- Buying power is the broker's limit on what you may buy.
- Leverage multiplies gains and losses equally, and borrowed money costs interest.
