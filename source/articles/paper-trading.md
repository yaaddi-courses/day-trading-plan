# Practicing risk-free: paper trading and demo accounts

**Covers:** paper trading, demo account

## Learning without paying for mistakes {#overview}
Every beginner makes errors: a wrong button, a wrong size, a plan abandoned in the heat of the moment. Making them with real money is the most expensive way to learn. A simulation lets you make the same errors for free, and learn the lessons before they cost anything.

## Paper trading {#paper-trading}
Paper trading is practicing trades with fake, simulated money on a real price feed, so mistakes cost nothing while you still learn. The name comes from the days when people wrote imaginary trades on paper. Today a platform does the bookkeeping: it fills your orders at the live prices and tracks the profit and loss.

## Demo account {#demo-account}
A demo account is a brokerage account funded with simulated money instead of real cash, used to paper trade a strategy at no risk. Most brokers and platforms offer one. It looks and works like a real account, with the same screens and order types.

## A worked example {#example}
A beginner opens a demo account with 25,000 simulated dollars and follows a plan for four weeks.

**Week 1: learn the tools.** He places orders of each type, practices the order entry window, sets a stop-loss and a take-profit, and cancels them. He makes about 30 small trades, only to learn where every button is. No result matters.

**Weeks 2 to 4: follow the plan.** Rules: risk 1% (250 simulated dollars) per trade, a maximum of 3 trades a day, stop after losing 2% in a day. He records every trade in a journal. After 20 trading days, he has 55 trades.

Results:

| Measure | Value |
|---|---|
| Trades | 55 |
| Winners | 24 |
| Win rate | 24 / 55 = 43.6% |
| Total result | +640 simulated dollars |
| Trades that broke a rule | 9 |

The plan looks positive: 640 on 25,000 is 2.6% in four weeks. But the sample is small. With 55 trades, luck can explain much of the result. A reasonable aim is 100 or more trades before drawing conclusions.

What paper trading shows well:
- Whether you can use the platform without errors.
- Whether you can follow your own rules for several weeks.
- Whether the strategy has a chance, roughly.

What it does not show well:
- **Emotions.** Losing 250 fake dollars does not hurt like losing 250 real ones. Discipline that holds in a simulation can break with real money.
- **Fill quality.** A demo often fills orders at the displayed price, and ignores slippage, so results look better than reality. In a fast market, real orders can fill noticeably worse.
- **Size.** Simulated orders of 5,000 shares fill instantly. Real orders of that size may not.

Because of these gaps, the usual path has three steps. First, a demo account until the rules are followed consistently. Second, a small real account, with a risk so small that mistakes are cheap, such as 10 dollars per trade. Third, a gradual increase as the results justify it. In the second step, the emotions appear, and you will learn if the plan survives them.

A good test for readiness: in the last 30 days of paper trading, did you follow every rule? If 9 of 55 trades broke a rule, as above, you are not ready. The aim is not profit, but discipline.

## A common mistake {#mistake}
Treating a demo account as a game and taking huge, reckless positions, then expecting the habits to disappear with real money. Practice as you mean to trade, with realistic size. Another mistake is trusting a very good simulated result without noting that fills, costs and emotions were missing.

## Key points {#key-points}
- Paper trading uses fake money on live prices. A demo account is the account you do it in.
- Use it to learn the tools and to test whether you can follow your plan.
- It hides emotions, slippage and real fill problems.
- Move to real money in small steps, with tiny risk.
