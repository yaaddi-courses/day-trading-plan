# Practicing risk-free: paper trading and demo accounts

**Covers:** paper trading, demo account

## Learning without paying for mistakes {#overview}
Every beginner makes errors: a wrong button, a wrong size, a plan dropped in the heat of the moment. Making them with real money is the most expensive way to learn. A simulation lets you make the same errors for free, so the lessons arrive before they cost you anything.

## Paper trading {#paper-trading}
Paper trading means practicing trades with pretend money against real prices. The name comes from the days when people wrote their imaginary trades on paper. Today a platform does the bookkeeping for you. It fills your orders at the live prices and tracks the profit and loss, so your mistakes cost nothing while you still learn from them.

## Demo account {#demo-account}
A demo account is the place where you paper trade. It looks and works like a real brokerage account, with the same screens and order types, but it is funded with simulated money. Most brokers and platforms offer one.

## A worked example {#example}
Imagine you open a demo account with 25,000 simulated dollars and follow a plan for four weeks.

**Week 1: learn the tools.** You place orders of each type, practice the order entry window, set a stop-loss and a take-profit, and cancel them. You make about 30 small trades, only to learn where every button is. No result matters this week.

**Weeks 2 to 4: follow the plan.** Your rules are to risk 1% (250 simulated dollars) per trade, take at most 3 trades a day, and stop for the day after losing 2%. You record every trade in a journal. After 20 trading days, you have 55 trades.

Your results:

| Measure | Value |
|---|---|
| Trades | 55 |
| Winners | 24 |
| Win rate | 24 / 55 = 43.6% |
| Total result | +640 simulated dollars |
| Trades that broke a rule | 9 |

The plan looks positive: 640 on 25,000 is 2.6% in four weeks. But the sample is small, and with 55 trades luck can explain much of the result. A reasonable aim is 100 trades or more before you draw conclusions.

Paper trading shows some things well:
- Whether you can use the platform without errors.
- Whether you can follow your own rules for several weeks.
- Roughly, whether the strategy has a chance.

It shows other things poorly:
- **Emotions.** Losing 250 fake dollars does not hurt like losing 250 real ones. Discipline that holds in a simulation can break with real money.
- **Fill quality.** A demo often fills orders at the displayed price and ignores slippage, so results look better than reality. In a fast market, real orders can fill noticeably worse.
- **Size.** Simulated orders of 5,000 shares fill instantly. Real orders of that size may not.

Because of these gaps, the usual path has three steps. First, use a demo account until you follow your rules consistently. Second, move to a small real account, with a risk so small that mistakes are cheap, such as 10 dollars per trade. Third, increase the size gradually as the results justify it. The emotions appear in the second step, and that is where you learn whether your plan survives them.

Here is a good test of readiness: in your last 30 days of paper trading, did you follow every rule? If 9 of 55 trades broke a rule, as above, you are not ready yet. The aim at this stage is discipline, not profit.

## A common mistake {#mistake}
A common slip is to treat a demo account as a game and take huge, reckless positions, then expect the habits to vanish when real money arrives. Practice the way you mean to trade, with a realistic size. Another slip is to trust a very good simulated result without remembering that fills, costs and emotions were missing from it.

## Key points {#key-points}
- Paper trading uses pretend money on live prices. A demo account is the account you do it in.
- Use it to learn the tools and to test whether you can follow your plan.
- It hides emotions, slippage and real fill problems.
- Move to real money in small steps, with a tiny risk.
