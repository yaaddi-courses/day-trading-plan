# Exchanges, ticker symbols and trading sessions

**Covers:** exchange, ticker symbol, AAPL, NYSE, trading session, pre-market, after-hours

## Where and when shares change hands {#overview}
Shares are not sold in a shop. They change hands in organized marketplaces, during set hours, and each company is identified by a short code. Before you place any trade, you need to know where it happens, how a stock is named, and which hours are the usual ones and which are riskier.

## Exchange {#exchange}
An exchange is the marketplace where stocks are actually bought and sold, such as the New York Stock Exchange or Nasdaq. It sets the rules for who may trade, which companies may list their shares, and how buyers and sellers are matched. A company chooses an exchange to list on, and its stock trades there. The United States has several exchanges, and one stock can trade on more than one of them.

## NYSE {#nyse}
NYSE stands for the New York Stock Exchange, one of the largest in the world. Many long-established companies are listed there, and its trading floor in New York is one of the best-known pictures of the stock market. Today most of its trading is electronic.

## Ticker symbol {#ticker-symbol}
Imagine typing a company's full name into every order. It would be slow, and two names could look alike. So each stock gets a short code of one to five letters, called the ticker symbol, which identifies it on an exchange.

## AAPL {#aapl}
AAPL is the ticker symbol of Apple. Lessons often use it as an example, because it is well known and heavily traded. Enter AAPL on a trading screen and you get Apple's shares and nothing else. Two companies never share a symbol on the same exchange.

## Trading session {#trading-session}
A trading session is a block of time in which trading takes place. The regular session of the US stock market runs from 9:30 in the morning to 4:00 in the afternoon, Eastern Time, on weekdays that are not holidays. That is 6.5 hours, and most of the day's trading happens in it.

## Pre-market {#pre-market}
Pre-market is the session before the official open. Many platforms allow trading from as early as 4:00 in the morning Eastern Time. It is usually thinner and choppier than regular hours. Fewer people trade, so prices can jump on small orders. News released overnight often causes the first big moves here.

## After-hours {#after-hours}
After-hours is the session after the official close, commonly until 8:00 in the evening Eastern Time. It has the same character: thin and choppy. Companies often publish earnings reports right after the close, so many stocks make big moves in this session before most people have had time to react.

## A worked example {#example}
Suppose you read this line in a news feed: "AAPL rose 3% in after-hours trading on NYSE-listed peers' results." Let's read it piece by piece.

1. AAPL tells you the stock: Apple.
2. After-hours tells you the time: after 4:00 pm Eastern, when few people are trading.
3. The 3% move happened in a thin session, so some of it may reverse when the regular session opens at 9:30 the next morning.

Now count the hours. The regular session has 6.5 hours (9:30 to 16:00). Pre-market, from 4:00 to 9:30, adds 5.5 hours. After-hours, from 16:00 to 20:00, adds 4. Together that is 16 hours of possible trading in a day, and only 6.5 of them, about 41%, belong to the regular session. The rest are the riskier hours.

| Session | Typical hours (Eastern) | Character |
|---|---|---|
| Pre-market | 4:00 to 9:30 | Thin, news-driven |
| Regular | 9:30 to 16:00 | Deepest, most trading |
| After-hours | 16:00 to 20:00 | Thin, earnings-driven |

Prices outside the regular session can mislead. A stock that "jumps 8% pre-market" on three small trades may open only 2% higher once thousands of traders arrive.

Many beginners choose to trade only in the regular session at first, for practical reasons: the most people are present, prices are steadier, and the tools work best.

## A common mistake {#mistake}
A common slip is to trade in pre-market or after-hours because of a headline, without noticing how thin the market is. A small order can move the price a lot, and leaving the position may be hard. Another slip is mixing up two companies with similar tickers. A quick look at the company name on the screen prevents it.

## Key points {#key-points}
- An exchange, such as the NYSE, is where stocks trade. A ticker symbol, such as AAPL, names one stock.
- The regular US session runs from 9:30 to 16:00 Eastern.
- Pre-market and after-hours are thinner and choppier.
- News moves are often exaggerated outside the regular session.
