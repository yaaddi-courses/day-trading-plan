# Exchanges, ticker symbols and trading sessions

**Covers:** exchange, ticker symbol, AAPL, NYSE, trading session, pre-market, after-hours

## Where and when shares change hands {#overview}
Shares are not sold in a shop. They are bought and sold on organized marketplaces, at set hours, under short codes that identify each company. Before placing any trade, you need to know where it happens, how a stock is named, and which hours are usual and which are risky.

## Exchange {#exchange}
An exchange is a marketplace where stocks are actually bought and sold, such as the New York Stock Exchange or Nasdaq. It sets the rules for who may trade, which companies may list their shares and how trades are matched. A company chooses an exchange to list on, and its stock then trades there. In the United States there are several exchanges, and one stock can trade on more than one of them.

## NYSE {#nyse}
NYSE is the New York Stock Exchange, one of the largest in the world. It lists many long-established companies, and its trading floor in New York is one of the best-known images of the stock market. Today, most of its trading is electronic.

## Ticker symbol {#ticker-symbol}
A ticker symbol is the short letter code that identifies a particular stock on an exchange. Typing a company's full name into an order would be slow and ambiguous, so each stock gets a code of one to five letters.

## AAPL {#aapl}
AAPL is the ticker symbol of Apple. It is a common example in lessons, because it is well known and heavily traded. Entering AAPL in a trading screen brings up Apple's shares and nothing else. Two different companies never share a symbol on the same exchange.

## Trading session {#trading-session}
A trading session is the block of time during which trading happens. The regular session of the US stock market runs from 9:30 in the morning to 4:00 in the afternoon, Eastern Time, on weekdays that are not holidays. That is 6.5 hours. Most of the day's trades happen in this window.

## Pre-market {#pre-market}
Pre-market is the trading session before the official open. Many platforms allow trading from as early as 4:00 in the morning Eastern Time. It is usually thinner and choppier than regular hours: fewer people trade, so prices can jump on small orders. Company news released overnight often causes the first big moves here.

## After-hours {#after-hours}
After-hours is the session after the official close, commonly until 8:00 in the evening Eastern Time. It has the same character: thin and choppy. Earnings reports are often published just after the close, so after-hours is where many stocks make big moves before most people can react.

## A worked example {#example}
Read this line from a news feed: "AAPL rose 3% in after-hours trading on NYSE-listed peers' results."

1. AAPL tells you the stock: Apple.
2. After-hours tells you the time: after 4:00 pm Eastern. Few people are trading.
3. The 3% move happened in the thin session, so it may be partly reversed when the regular session opens at 9:30 the next morning.

Count the hours. The regular session has 6.5 hours (9:30 to 16:00). Pre-market from 4:00 to 9:30 adds 5.5 hours, and after-hours from 16:00 to 20:00 adds 4. Together that is 16 hours of possible trading in a day, but only 6.5 of them, about 41%, are the regular session. The rest are the riskier ones.

| Session | Typical hours (Eastern) | Character |
|---|---|---|
| Pre-market | 4:00 to 9:30 | Thin, news-driven |
| Regular | 9:30 to 16:00 | Deepest, most trading |
| After-hours | 16:00 to 20:00 | Thin, earnings-driven |

Prices outside the regular session can be misleading. A stock that "jumps 8% pre-market" on three small trades may open only 2% higher once thousands of traders arrive.

Beginners are usually advised to trade only in the regular session first. The reasons are practical: the most people are present, prices are steadier, and the tools work best.

## A common mistake {#mistake}
Trading in pre-market or after-hours because of a headline, without noticing how thin the market is. A small order can move the price a lot, and getting out may be hard. Another mistake is confusing two companies with similar tickers, which a quick check of the name on the screen prevents.

## Key points {#key-points}
- An exchange, such as the NYSE, is where stocks trade. A ticker symbol, such as AAPL, names one stock.
- The regular US session is 9:30 to 16:00 Eastern.
- Pre-market and after-hours are thinner and choppier.
- News moves are often exaggerated outside the regular session.
